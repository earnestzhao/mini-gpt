# What Are the Main Components of a Complete LLM?

Ready to get started? Let's begin by looking at the basic structure and components of a complete LLM.

## Talking to a Friend

Imagine this scenario. One day, you're chatting with a friend. He's incredibly patient, always happy to answer your questions, and never gets tired of them. He's also knowledgeable about a wide range of subjects, so there's hardly a topic he can't say something about.

You casually ask him a question: What kind of animal has feathers?

Without a second thought, he replies: An animal with feathers is a bird.

It all happens so quickly and naturally that neither of you stops to think about what actually happened in between.

So, let's take a closer look. First, you ask your question: What kind of animal has feathers?

To understand the sentence, your friend generally needs to understand what the individual words mean and how they work together. So, for the sake of our example, he first breaks the sentence down into words.

For example, he might split it into: "What", "kind of", "animal", "has", "feathers", "?".

In a real conversation, your friend probably wouldn't explicitly separate out punctuation. But that information is still there: he can tell from your intonation that you're asking a question, which is roughly equivalent to recognizing the question mark. We don't need to worry too much about that detail.

Second, he needs to understand what those words refer to. If he doesn't know what they mean, he can't answer your question.

Take "feathers", for example. He needs to know what feathers are in the real world. If he thinks "feathers" and "stone" mean roughly the same thing, he won't be able to give the correct answer.

Third, he needs to figure out what kind of information you're asking for. Clearly, the answer should be the name of an animal or a category of animals.

In other words, he needs to recognize that you're asking for an animal or a category of animals. He draws on his knowledge within that general semantic space to come up with an answer.

Fourth, he speaks his final answer: "An animal with feathers is a bird."

There's one more important condition. If your friend were a newborn baby, he wouldn't be able to give a meaningful answer. His ability to answer comes from years of growing up, learning, and gaining knowledge and experience.

## Talking to an LLM

Now think about this: what if we replaced that knowledgeable, endlessly patient, and reliable friend with an LLM?

Although an LLM's actual computational mechanisms are different from those of the human brain, we can divide the two processes into several similar steps from an information-processing perspective. The difference is that an LLM performs these steps using a series of functional modules rather than a human brain.

The first step, breaking a sentence into words, is handled by the **Tokenizer** module.

The second step, understanding what those words refer to, is handled by the **Embedding** module.

The third step, figuring out what information to provide, is handled by the **Transformer** module.

The fourth step, producing the final answer, is handled by the **Generator** module.

And the **Trainer** module helps the LLM develop the ability to answer questions effectively.

We can therefore divide a complete LLM system into five main functional modules: **Tokenizer, Embedding, Transformer, Generator, and Trainer**. The data flow between them is illustrated below:

```text
           +-------------+      +-------------+      +---------------+      +-------------+
Input ---> |  Tokenizer  | ---> |  Embedding  | ---> |  Transformer  | ---> |  Generator  | ---> Output
           +-------------+      +-------------+      +---------------+      +-------------+
                                       ^                 ^       |
                                       |                 |       |          +-------------+
                                       |                 |       +--------> |   Trainer   |
                                       |                 |                  +-------------+
                                       |                 |                         |
                                       +-----------------+-------------------------+
```

As the diagram shows, the Generator and Trainer belong to two different pipelines. Training and inference (text generation) are two different processes, but they share model components such as Embedding and Transformer. An untrained LLM (like a newborn baby or a child who is still learning) cannot yet answer questions effectively. A trained LLM (like our knowledgeable, patient, and reliable friend) can.

Hopefully, the analogy above makes intuitive sense. Next, let's use slightly more technical language to briefly explain what each module does and how it works.

### Tokenizer

The Tokenizer's main job is to split continuous text into the smallest units the model processes, called **Tokens**, and convert them into numeric identifiers, called **Token IDs**. It acts as a bridge between human language and the machine world, determining the smallest units at which the model processes language. Different tokenization methods determine how an LLM breaks down and processes human language.

For our earlier question, "What kind of animal has feathers?", a Tokenizer might produce the following Tokens and Token IDs:

| Token ID | Token |
| -------- | ----- |
| 104 | What |
| 213 | kind of |
| 347 | animal |
| 458 | has |
| 577 | feathers |
| 860 | ? |

### Embedding

After tokenization, we have a sequence of Tokens and Token IDs. But Tokens and their numeric IDs alone don't establish semantic information or relationships between those IDs.

Returning to our example, the LLM now has the sequence of Token IDs: 104, 213, 347, 458, 577, 860. As far as these IDs alone are concerned, they're just numbers, with no inherent semantic distinction. Suppose the Token ID for "stone" is 742. From the IDs alone, the LLM has no way to tell how "feathers" (577) differs from "stone" (742), let alone answer our question.

This is where Embedding comes in. We can think of Embedding as mapping Tokens into a high-dimensional semantic representation space. In this space, each Token is represented by a high-dimensional dense vector. For example:

| Token ID | Token | Embedding |
| -------- | ----- | --------- |
| 104 | What | [0.0626304, 0.126459, -0.0582043, ...] |
| 213 | kind of | [0.0608181, 0.0613651, -0.0755621, ...] |
| 347 | animal | [-0.174222, -0.124878, -0.056266, ...] |
| 458 | has | [-0.0682421, -0.100038, -0.00864957, ...] |
| 577 | feathers | [-0.173733, -0.100062, -0.0194214, ...] |
| 742 | stone | [0.173759, 0.00167136, 0.0729713, ...] |
| 633 | bird | [-0.206716, -0.145065, -0.015243, ...] |

Through training, these vectors can carry semantic information associated with Tokens, along with other linguistic features such as syntax and word forms.

A high-dimensional vector can be viewed as a coordinate in a high-dimensional space. In this semantic space, words with similar meanings are placed near one another, allowing the model to capture how closely related different words are. With appropriate training, the model learns certain semantic relationships among these Tokens. We'll discuss how those relationships are represented in vectors, and how vector similarity can be measured, in later articles.

We usually cannot directly explain what each individual component of a vector (corresponding to a dimension or coordinate) represents, but the model can use these vectors in subsequent computations.

### Transformer

This is the core of an LLM. As discussed earlier, the Transformer helps the LLM figure out what information it needs to provide. To do this, it uses a particularly important mechanism called **Self-Attention**. With Self-Attention, the Transformer can compute relationships between different Tokens in a sequence and process their information in context. It not only handles the meanings of individual words but also uses context to interpret ambiguous words: for example, whether "Apple" refers to a fruit or a technology company.

Think of the Transformer as a powerful processing plant: it brings together fragmented semantic vectors, extracts important contextual information and identifies what that information suggests, and provides the features needed for subsequent text generation and model training.

In our example, Self-Attention allows the Transformer to connect information expressed by Tokens such as "feathers" and "animal", use the context to determine what we're asking, and provide the basis for generating "bird" as the answer.

### Generator

The Generator uses the contextual features extracted by the Transformer to predict which Token is most likely to come next, or to generate the next Token according to a probability-based rule. It works **autoregressively**: it generates one Token, feeds that Token back into the input, and continues generating Tokens using the same probability-based rule until it produces a complete answer.

For our example, the Generator works roughly as follows:

| Current Input | Computation | Most Probable Next Token |
| ------------- | ----------- | ------------------------ |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? | Tokenizer, Embedding, Transformer | A: |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: | Embedding, Transformer | An |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An | Embedding, Transformer | animal |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal | Embedding, Transformer | with |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with | Embedding, Transformer | feathers |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with feathers | Embedding, Transformer | is |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with feathers is | Embedding, Transformer | a |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with feathers is a | Embedding, Transformer | bird |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with feathers is a bird | Embedding, Transformer | . |
| Q: What kind of animal has feathers? A: An animal with feathers is a bird. | Embedding, Transformer | `<EOS>` |

### Trainer

During training, the Trainer feeds large amounts of data into the model. It uses the computation described above (the **forward pass**) to calculate the error, or **Loss**, then uses **Backpropagation** to compute gradients and, in turn, update model parameters, including the Transformer parameters and the values in the Embedding vectors.

With the help of the Trainer, a model can grow from a baby that makes random guesses into a knowledgeable and reliable friend, capable of giving meaningful answers to our questions.

In our example, before the model is properly trained, it might produce an answer like "Feathers and stones are animals" or "The weather is lovely today". As training progresses, the model typically learns patterns in the data, and its training loss tends to decrease overall. With sufficient and effective training, the model can provide reasonable and useful answers to many questions.

This article introduced five main modules, **Tokenizer, Embedding, Transformer, Generator, and Trainer**, their roles, and the data flow shown in the diagram above.
